Red Alder

Alnus rubra Bong. · Betulaceae

🌿 NativeBC CDC: YellowTreeZone 4a–8b🦋 118 Lepidoptera hosts

Overview

Summary

Deciduous monecious tree up to 25 m tall with smooth grey lichened bark; inner bark turns rusty red when cut; soft, spongy orange cambium; male/female catkins; small winged nutlets in brown cone-like fruits (t’um’ekw’un). Found in woodlands, forests, floodplains, streambanks, clearcuts, slides, creeks, beaches, and riparian zones across lowland and montane zones, this taxon is common in coastal British Columbia, extending north to southeast Alaska and south to California.

Botanical Description

This versatile deciduous shrub or tree can reach a prominent height of 25 meters. Its bark, often adorned with lichens, presents a distinctive tapestry of yellowish-brown and grey-white splotches. The smooth leaves, with their unique outward-pointing teeth and rolled-under margins, add a touch of distinction. Before the leaves fully emerge, the tree unveils a compact display of male and female catkins.

Name Etymology

Alnus is Latin for alder, and rubra means red, referring to its reddish bark or wood.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Found in woodlands, forests, floodplains, streambanks, clearcuts, slides, creeks, beaches, and riparian zones across lowland and montane zones, this taxon is common in coastal British Columbia, extending north to southeast Alaska and south to California. It is frequently associated with mixed evergreen and redwood forests, often forming communities with red alder trees. Red alder itself is primarily observed in moist areas within 200 kilometers of the Pacific Coast from Alaska to southern California at elevations below 762 meters, and ranges along streams and lakes from the Yukon Territory and British Columbia south through the Rocky Mountain region to Colorado and New Mexico, and along the Sierra Nevada to Lower California. Due to increased human disturbance such as logging, it has also successfully colonized upland areas.

Succession in most local stands of the Alnus-Athyrium association is prevented by regular sliding and slumping of water-saturated soil- and parent material. Successional stages, converted to agriculture.

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Woodland / Shade; Rock Garden; Rain Garden / Wetland; Coastal / Bluff; Hedgerow / Thicket

Key Features

Pollinator Magnet; Butterfly / Moth Host; Bird Forage; Deer Resistant; Nitrogen Fixer; Edible

Soil Preferences

Red alder has two adaptations that allow the trees to colonize bare infertile substrates: (1) the roots have nodules that house symbiotic bacteria which fix nitrogen from the air contained in the pores of the soil, and (2) the seeds need sunlight to germinate. Actinomycetes (filamentous bacteria) in the genus Frankia invade alders through their root hairs and stimulate cell division, forming nodules on the roots Species of Frankia remove nitrogen from the air and ‘fix’ it in a form useful to plants. Red alder provides a home for the actinomycete, which in turn ‘leaks’ some of the nitrogen, making it available for the alder.

Moisture Notes

Thrives in seasonally saturated soils, riparian benches, or poorly drained depressions.

Spread Behaviour

Woody crown and lateral root system

Root Formation

Deep lateral / Taproot / Woody burl

Mycorrhizal Association

Dual Ectomycorrhizal (EcM) & Actinorhizal Nitrogen-Fixing (Frankia root nodules)

Competition / Allelopathy

Symbiotic nitrogen-fixer that elevates site fertility and accelerates canopy closure; in low-nutrient Garry oak or alpine meadows, space carefully so vigorous nitrogen responders do not crowd slow forbs.

Erosion & Slope Control

Early colonizer of open areas; used as bioindicator for ozone pollution (leaves turn purple).

Pruning & Management

If desired, red alder trees can be pruned for shape when young. Follow up pruning is minimal and consists of removing any suckers that may form as well as removing dead wood or crossing branches.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

The inner bark was often dried, ground into a powder and then used as a thickener in soups or mixed with cereals when making bread. Spring inner bark eaten as food (Straits Salish). Inner cambium (sxam'us) scraped and eaten fresh.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Inner cambium (sxam'us) scraped and eaten fresh; sweet runny sap; cones placed under baby pillows for strength.

Material & Technology

Stems/roots used for basketry. Inner bark used as red-orange-brown dye for baskets, canoes, fibers, and fishnets (making them invisible to fish). Wood used as fuel for smoking fish.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Inner bark decoction used internally as a purgative and for respiratory illness/tuberculosis, and externally for sores, wounds, and skin conditions. Bark decoction/boiled for purgative, wound remedy, TB. Bark solution used as skin wash for infections and rashes.

Propagation & Seeds

Stratification & Dormancy

Red alder prefers moist, well-drained, deep sandy loams (Labadie 1978) and full sun. Quick growing red alders can be planted with slower growing trees such as oak to provide quick screening (Lowry 1999).

Sowing Protocol

Red Alder has female cones and male catkins on the same tree (monecious). The female cones are what produce the winged nutlets that fall to the ground in winter. You can collect the cones and dry them just a little to release the seeds or collect the seeds directly off the ground.

Propagation Methods

Red alder prefers moist, well-drained, deep sandy loams and full sun.
