Overview

Summary

Deciduous Shrub or tree, 1-7 m height with white flowers and edible purple-black berries; smooth grey/brown branches 9; flexible, strong wood. Inhabiting woodlands, thickets, meadows, dry to mesic open rocky slopes, bluffs, gullies, forest margins, and open forests from the lowland to subalpine zones, this species is common throughout British Columbia, particularly in central and southern regions east of the Coast-Cascade Mountains.

Botanical Description

A large white-flowering shrub with edible blue berries. Flowers attract pollinators.

Name Etymology

Amelanchier is derived from a Provençal name for the plant, and alnifolia means alder-leaved.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabiting woodlands, thickets, meadows, dry to mesic open rocky slopes, bluffs, gullies, forest margins, and open forests from the lowland to subalpine zones, this species is common throughout British Columbia, particularly in central and southern regions east of the Coast-Cascade Mountains. Its broader distribution extends north to Alaska, east to Alberta, and south to California, Nebraska, North Dakota, Colorado, and Arizona. It ranges from lower-elevation coniferous forests up to timberline, riparian zones, open or shaded disturbed sites, open woodlands, mountain shrublands, coulees, dry bluffs, and oak meadows, and is distributed along the Pacific coast from Alaska to California, eastward to Utah, Colorado, Iowa, Minnesota, Ontario, and Quebec, and north through the plains and prairies into the Northwest Territories of Canada.

Soil Preferences

Moisture Notes

Prefers evenly moist, well-drained soils without standing winter water.

Spread Behaviour

Rhizomatous colony- or thicket-former

Root Formation

Rhizomatous

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic but vigorous clonal spreader (Rhizomatous); forms thickets that outcompete slow-establishing forbs—allow generous spacing or use root barriers in tight borders.

Erosion & Slope Control

Excellent for erosion control; soil and slope stabilization, massive root crown, horizontal/vertical rhizomes; drought, salt, and clay tolerant.

Pruning & Management

Saskatoon in forests is fire-dependent, occurring in forests with fire regimes varying from frequent, low-severity fire (low-elevation forests) to infrequent, severe fire. It may persist in the understory for decades but eventually dies out with fire exclusion and canopy closure. After top-kill by light-to moderate-severity fire, saskatoon sprouts usually arise from the root crown or from shallowly buried rhizomes; sprouts arise from deeply buried rhizomes after even the most intense fire. Sasktoon cover and biomass production in western Montana may generally increase after fires in Douglas-fir/ninebark habitats, but browsing pressure from big game may slow the recovery.

Fall & Winter Interest

Persistent fruit, hips, cones, or dried seedheads add late-season visual interest and winter wildlife forage.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Edible fruit. Berries dried on racks for winter food. Flexible wood steamed to make permanent bends for dip net hoops and baby boards.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Berries; Edible berries; used in pies, jams, syrups, wine, pemmican.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Branch tea for colds, post-childbirth recovery. Wood and bark used for medicine. Branches used as oil additive for lung problems, coughs, chest pain, muscle spasms, pinched nerves, diarrhoea, fever, flu, colds.

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

Propagation Methods

Saskatoon has been artificially crossed with other species of serviceberry, and similar hybridization and intergradation also occurs in the field, particularly between saskatoon and low serviceberry (Amelanchier humilis Wieg.) Cultivars sold as Amelanchier alnifolia may actually be hybrids between A. alnifolia and other species of Amelanchier. Saskatoon reproduces from seed but more commonly by sprouting from the root crown and rhizomes and by layering.
