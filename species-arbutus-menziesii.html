Pacific Madrone

Arbutus menziesii Pursh. · Ericaceae

Overview

Summary

Evergreen tree 6 to 30 m tall; cinnamon red thin smooth bark peeling in papery flakes away from trunk; thick glossy dark green oval leaves; fragrant white-to-pink bell flowers; scarlet red mealy berries. Inhabiting woodlands, rocky slopes, dry open forests on coarse or shallow soils in the lowland and montane zones; common in extreme southern BC and extending south to Baja CA; found in wooded slopes and canyons within oak, redwood, mixed evergreen, and chaparral communities at elevations of 100-1500 m, ranging from southern CA to British Columbia.

Botanical Description

Evergreen tree up to 30 m tall with characteristic peeling reddish bark; alternate dark waxy green oval leaves (15 cm); hanging clusters of white bell flowers; bright small red berries. Pacific madrone is an evergreen broadleaf tree of the Heath family (Ericaceae), native to the northwestern Coast Ranges of North America. Reaching heights of 6 to 30 m tall (6-30 m, or 6m to 30m or more depending on environmental conditions), trees feature single or multiple trunks from the base with rounded, spreading crowns, or a more shrublike habit. Young bark is chartreuse and smooth, aging to thin, smooth, deep brownish-red or cinnamon red bark that peels in large papery flakes away from the trunk and young branches to reveal an attractive, satiny green surface beneath that darkens with time to deep red. In midsummer, the exfoliated bark and shed leaves in shades of red to orange form a distinctive colorful carpet beneath the tree canopy (Saunders 1923).

Name Etymology

Arbutus is Latin for strawberry tree, and menziesii honors Archibald Menzies, a Scottish botanist.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabiting woodlands, rocky slopes, dry open forests on coarse or shallow soils in the lowland and montane zones; common in extreme southern BC and extending south to Baja CA; found in wooded slopes and canyons within oak, redwood, mixed evergreen, and chaparral communities at elevations of 100-1500 m, ranging from southern CA to British Columbia. Pacific madrone typically occurs in mixed stands and is native to the West Coast of North America, with occasional populations in the middle elevations of the Sierra Nevada Range.

Climax (Inferred). Pioneer species following landslides or clearing.

Fire Ecology

Pacific madrone depends on periodic fire to reduce the shading resulting from the closing conifer overstory. Fire suppression causes Pacific madrone to be out-competed by species that can better tolerate shade.

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Woodland / Shade; Rock Garden; Coastal / Bluff

Key Features

Soil Preferences

These trees are adapted to humid coastal sites as well as dry foothill slopes and canyons in areas with dry summers and mild winters. They grow on soils with low nitrogen and low moisture content. Dry wooded slopes, canyons, oak savannas, and rocky coastal bluffs; prefers well-drained, low-nitrogen, moisture-deficient soils.

Moisture Notes

Requires sharp drainage and winter precipitation followed by dry summers.

Spread Behaviour

Deep spreading root system with woody basal burl capable of post-fire resprouting

Root Formation

Woody Burl & Spreading Roots

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbutoid Mycorrhiza (Ectomycorrhizal & Ericoid forest fungal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Forms tight ericaceous root mats and phenolic leaf litter that gradually acidifies surface humus and slows annual weed invasion; compatible with acid-tolerant woodland companions.

Erosion & Slope Control

Prevents erosion on disturbed sites via widely spreading roots and vigorous re-sprouting from underground burls; facilitates Douglas-fir growth; Slope & bank stabilization.

Pruning & Management

Within managed conifer stands, Pacific madrone has been considered a “weedy” tree species. This is because it can out-compete replanting of timber species because of its ability to resprout from its stump or burl after disturbance. Interestingly, there is some evidence that Pacific madrone can facilitate growth of Douglas fir at some sites. In natural areas, the situation is reversed from that of timber production areas.

Fall & Winter Interest

Distinctive winter bark and stem colour provide strong cold-season architectural interest.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Edible fruit. The Salinan, Miwok, Pomo, and other California tribes have long used the berries of Pacific madrone for food and to make cider. Berries eaten fresh, made into unfermented cider, or cooked and dried for later use.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Berries eaten fresh, cooked, dried, or cider.

Material & Technology

The dense, fine-grained, wood is heavy and brittle. Bark used for toys and wood carved into tools. Fine wood used for carving, utensils, and gunpowder charcoal.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Leaves or inner bark chewed for toothache and stomach problems. Bark boiled into tea for colds and sore throats. Leaves chewed to reduce stomach pain and cramps.

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Insect pests include aphids, caterpillars, woodborers, and Madrone psyllid. The trees are susceptible to several fungal infections, which cause leaf diseases, root rot, and crown rot. Pacific madrone is also affected by “sudden oak death” caused by the introduced fungus, Phytophthora. | Recorded insect/host associations (beees sheet): Coptodisca arbutiella: in leaves; Marmara arbutiella: in leaves

Propagation & Seeds

Seed Collection

Pacific madrone is a prolific seed producer, which produces seed every year from the age of three to five years. One pound of seeds will produce approximately 1,000 plants. Seedlings grow slowly and are highly susceptible to mortality. To start from seed, gather the fruits from the trees when they are ripe—generally from October to December.

Propagation Methods

These trees should only be planted in very well drained soils in areas with non-alkaline water. Although generally started from seed, Pacific madrone may also be propagated from cuttings, grafting, or layers. For best germination, use seeds that have been stratified by pre-chilling for one to two months at 2 to 5°C. Propagated by seed (stratified 1–2 months at 2–5°C) or sprouting from underground burls after fire/cutting.
