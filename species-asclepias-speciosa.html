Showy Milkweed

Asclepias speciosa Torr. · Apocynaceae

Overview

Summary

Perennial forb with hairy stems, 0.6–1.5 m tall; velvety opposite leaves to 15.2 cm; rose-colored star-shaped flowers in fuzzy umbel; horn-shaped fruits with silky seeds; sticky sap. Moist to mesic roadsides, ditches, streamsides, meadows, and grasslands in the steppe and montane zones; common in south-central and southeastern British Columbia; east to Manitoba and south to Missouri, Texas, and California; tolerant of moist soils in prairies, meadows, roadsides, streams, and ditches; drought-tolerant; western United States and Canada.

Botanical Description

A member of the Milkweed Family (Asclepiadaceae), showy milkweed (Asclepias speciosa) is a hairy perennial forb and herb arising from a rhizome, reaching 0.6–1.5 m tall with erect to ascending stems 0.4-1.2 m tall that are woolly, greyish, or glabrous below, yielding sticky sap and milky juice. The velvety opposite leaves measure to 6 in (10-20 cm long), being simple, fleshy, persistent, and short-petioled with oblong or oval to elliptic or ovate blades, rounded or heart-shaped at the base (rarely cordate and clasping). Asclepias flowers resemble crowns arranged in fuzzy umbels, displaying rose-colored, star-shaped petals with a rose-purple reflexed corolla and hoods elevated above the corolla in pink, aging yellow, with horns exerted beyond the hoods. Fire Ecology

Both milkweed and dogbane were burned in the fall by California Indian tribes to eliminate dead stalks and stimulate new growth. Burning causes new growth to have taller, straighter stems with longer fibers.

Garden Uses

Soil Preferences

Prairies, meadows, roadsides, stream banks, and ditches; prefers moist soil, highly drought-tolerant. Dry to moist loamy soils.

Moisture Notes

Adapted to well-drained winter-wet soils with prolonged summer dry periods.

Spread Behaviour

Slowly spreading rhizomatous clump

Root Formation

Rhizomatous

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic moderate clonal spreader (Rhizomatous); weaves steadily through mixed groundcover matrices without harsh chemical inhibition.

Pruning & Management

When used for fiber, milkweed is collected in the autumn after the leaves have begun to senesce or dry up. Plants grow in places that have been disturbed such as road grading, construction sites, logging landing fills, and etc. Pete Bunting (pers. Comm. 2-9-1999) says, "In the fall when the milkweed has dried I check to see if they will break off at the ground line. The plants are usually a yellow tan to gray depending on how long they have dried."

Fall & Winter Interest

Persistent fruit, hips, cones, or dried seedheads add late-season visual interest and winter wildlife forage.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Edible shoots, buds, flowers, young seed pods, ground seeds. Pueblo people ate green milkweed pods and uncooked roots from one of the species that forms fleshy tubers underground. The young shoots, stems, flower buds, immature fruits, and roots of showy milkweed were boiled and eaten as a vegetable by various indigenous groups of eastern and mid-western America. In some areas the young leaves and stems were used as greens.

Material & Technology

Stems used as cordage. Fibers from the stems of milkweed have been identified in prehistoric textiles in the Pueblo region. Tewa-speaking people of the Rio Grande still make string and rope from these fibers.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

People have used milkweed for fiber, food, and medicine all over the United States and southern Canada. Sap used as antiseptic for cuts, ringworm, corns, calluses (Cheyenne). Seed decoction for snakebite (Paiute).

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Propagation & Seeds

Stratification & Dormancy

Collect seeds after pods have ripened, but before they have split open. The seeds are wind dispersed, so be careful when gathering to place in a paper or burlap bag to avoid seed lost.

Sowing Protocol

Seeds can be directly sewn into the ground in the fall.

Propagation Methods

Seeds and plants of selected Asclepias cultivars are available from many nurseries. It is best to plant species from your local area, adapted to the specific site conditions where the plants are to be grown.
