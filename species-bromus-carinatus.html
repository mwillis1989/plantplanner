Overview

Summary

Tall, robust native perennial meadow grass (30-100 cm) with soft broad foliage often showing purple hues. Found in deeper soil meadows and woodlands, growing in open woods and forests, shrublands, grasslands, meadows, and waste places.

Botanical Description

Tall, robust native perennial meadow grass (30-100 cm) with soft broad foliage often showing purple hues. California brome is a native, cool-season perennial bunchgrass that lives 3 to 5 years, growing 60 to 120 cm tall (overall foliage range 30-100 cm). It features deep, widespread, quickly growing fibrous roots. Young plants are erect, but older stems grow along the ground with only the apical tips remaining erect (decumbent).

Name Etymology

Bromus comes from Greek bromos (oats), and carinatus means keeled, referring to its boat-shaped spikelets.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Found in deeper soil meadows and woodlands, growing in open woods and forests, shrublands, grasslands, meadows, and waste places. It is closely associated with pine dropseed, bracken fern, corn lily, dwarf purple monkey flower, mountain muhly, and Rocky Mountain iris, and shares dominance in plant communities such as coastal prairie, northern coastal scrub, sagebrush steppes, aspen forests, oak woodlands, and Douglas-fir forests. Its distribution extends from Alaska east to Ontario and south to Illinois, Texas, California, and northern Mexico, being native from the Pacific Coast to the Rocky Mountains and casually introduced in the Great Plains. California brome grows in open woods and forests, shrublands, grasslands, meadows, and waste places.

Fire Ecology

California brome is top-killed by fire, but appears to recover within a few years. Coverage of California brome is slightly reduced from pre-fire levels for several years after fire, then returns to pre-fire levels.

Soil Preferences

California brome grows well in a variety of soils including poorly drained types. It is most abundant in moderately moist, well-developed, deep, medium-textured soils. It tolerates soils in the pH range of 5.5 to 8.0.

Moisture Notes

Adapted to well-drained winter-wet soils with prolonged summer dry periods.

Pruning & Management

Although fair control of California brome by trifluralin and pronamide has been documented, it does not respond to most herbicides. It is reduced by heavy grazing and favored in moderate to light grazing. Sheep are more likely to kill plants by trampling them rather than overgrazing. Cattle are more likely to overgraze than trample the plants.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

They were often dried and ground into flour to make bread and other foods. Seeds were traditionally parched and ground into flour for bread.

Safety

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

The Western Society of Weed Science has listed California brome as having invasive characteristics. Dense stands can spread aggressively and crowd out surrounding vegetation if unmanaged.

Propagation & Seeds

Seed Collection

Seeds are collected between May 1 and September 10, depending on rate of maturation. Mature inflorescences turn from green-purple to brown and mature seeds are light brown-gray. Cleaning is not required for germination.

Stratification & Dormancy

Seed dormancy is usually absent in natural populations from low elevation so California brome can be fall or spring sown. Germination occurs in 10-14 days and seedling growth and plant development are rapid.

Sowing Protocol

Seeds have very high viability. Seedlings are fast-growing and have high establishment. Sow in fall or early spring for germination in the same season.

Propagation Methods

‘Southern Cal 1000’ Germplasm California brome (Bromus carinatus) was collected in 2006 in Orange County near Irvine Ranch at 305 m elevation on silty clay soils in a purple needlegrass community. It was selected from 28 accessions after a two-year field trial evaluation.
