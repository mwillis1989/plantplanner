Great Camas

Overview

Summary

Perennial herb 60-120 cm (or 20-60 cm) tall from edible bulb; long narrow grass-like basal leaves with fine waxy powder; spike-like cluster of creamy-white to deep blue-violet flowers; barrel-shaped/3-angled capsule with black seeds. Inhabiting vernally moist meadows in the lowland zone; locally frequent on southeastern Vancouver Island and the Gulf Islands, with a single report from the adjacent mainland (Sechelt Peninsula); ranging south to California.

Botanical Description

This prominent perennial emerges from a substantial bulb to reach heights of up to 100 cm. Its grass-like basal leaves create a lush foundation for the showy display of pale to deep blue flowers. These large, distinctive blooms, reaching up to 4 cm in length, form an elegant terminal raceme. As the flowers mature, their tepals twist together, adding a unique visual element.

Name Etymology

Genus Camassia; specific epithet leichtlinii honors a botanical collector or naturalist associated with Great Camas.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabiting vernally moist meadows in the lowland zone; locally frequent on southeastern Vancouver Island and the Gulf Islands, with a single report from the adjacent mainland (Sechelt Peninsula); ranging south to California. Great camas occurs in coastal oak meadows from southern British Columbia to California, west of the Cascades, as well as in moist savannahs, ditches, and seepages in the CDF zone, Salish Sea meadows, and the Willamette Valley, inhabiting dry and moist meadows from southern Vancouver Island to northern California, coast to the Cascades and Sierra Nevada below 3292 m, alongside wet meadows, woods, prairies, moist hillsides, streamsides, and riparian zones. Great camas grows in wet meadows, woods, prairies, moist hillsides, and streamside areas, often in ephemeral habitats that dry up by late spring. In western Oregon, its preferred habitat includes shady environments such as riparian zones or under moderately dense deciduous forest canopies, though it can inhabit more open areas elsewhere in its range.

Fire Ecology

Late summer field burning (where and when permitted) may improve stand vigor, reduce competition from brush and certain weeds, and aid in regeneration. Grassfires used by Indian population to maintain and expand oak parkland. Maintained and expanded by grassfires at expense of surrounding coniferous forests.

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Meadow; Woodland / Shade; Rock Garden; Coastal / Bluff; Hedgerow / Thicket

Key Features

Pollinator Magnet; Drought Tolerant; Erosion Control; Edible

Soil Preferences

Great camas is frequently found in areas with soil moisture persisting throughout most of the growing season. Bulb depth appears limited by shallow water tables, anoxic conditions, or restrictive layers. Dry to seasonally wet; 5.6 to 6.8 pH; tolerates clay soils; 0% CaCO3 tolerance.

Moisture Notes

Adapted to well-drained winter-wet soils with prolonged summer dry periods. (Wetland indicator: FACW.)

Spread Behaviour

Slowly multiplying clump from underground bulbs or corms

Root Formation

Bulb / Corm / Tuber

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic and non-aggressive (Bulb / Corm / Tuber); low root competition makes it safe for intimate mixed borders and diverse polyculture interplanting.

Erosion & Slope Control

Used for prairie restoration and ground cover; planted via seed mixes or bulb transplants to restore wet prairie/wetland habitats; Slope & bank stabilization.

Pruning & Management

Consistent soil moisture is required every spring, but the soil can be allowed to dry out soon after the pods mature or the leaves senesce (dry up and turn brown). Moderate soil nutrient levels are beneficial. In natural settings, minor soil disturbance (loosening, surface scarification) adjacent to existing specimens may enhance natural regeneration by seed; seedling recruitment is highest in areas that are bare when the seeds germinate in February.

Fall & Winter Interest

Emerges winter-to-spring before retreating into summer dormancy.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

According to Dr. Nancy Turner, TRM included the following: Ownership, demarcation, and inheritance of beds or patches, Clearing of rock, brush, and weedy vegetation, Harvesting bulbs after seeds were produced, during specific times of the year, Periodic field burning in summer after digging, Digging or “cultivation” to keep the soil loose, Sustainable harvest techniques, including partial, selective harvests and incidental or planned promotion of camas In some cases, sod removal then bulb removal followed by sod replacement, “Selective breeding” by transplanting “better” bulbs to the beds, colonization and reproduction, and Death camas bulbs (Zigadenus venenosus) were removed, so they wouldn’t accidentally be mistaken for the edible camas bulbs. Starchy bulb used as a staple food and trade item for Indigenous Peoples in the region for generations. Important root food cooked in pits/stovetops, sun-dried, in cakes, or soaked. The bulbs were dug with a pointed digging stick.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Bulb; Extensively cultivated staple food and trade item; bulbs steamed/roasted in pit-ovens for 24-48 hrs to convert inulin to fructose; dried, stored, eaten at feasts/potlatches; Important root food cooked in pits/stovetops, sun-dried, in cakes, or soaked; sweetener, flavoring; stalks/leaves for mattresses; Bulbs.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Nez Perce used boiled root juice mixed with honey as cough medicine. Prepared as cough medicine (boiled, juice strained and mixed with honey).

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Camas is favored as forage by deer, so fencing or repellents may be useful, particularly during the first growing season. Bulb predation by pocket gophers can be a substantial threat to stands if left unchecked.

Propagation & Seeds

Seed Collection

Great camas propagates easily from seed. It can be collected as soon as the pods mature (turn light brown) or split open to reveal the mature black seeds. Pods ripen from late May to July.

Stratification & Dormancy

Camas seed is dormant and typically requires 60-100 days of cold moist stratification (moist chilling at 1–4°C) for maximum germination (Bartow, 2015). Fall-sown seedbeds should be kept weed-free during early spring establishment.

Sowing Protocol

Great camas can be propagated from seeds or bulbs; shade tolerance, moisture requirements, and preferred planting depth may differ among specific phenotypes. Plants require irrigation or moist soil conditions to become established. Great camas can be difficult to establish in California.

Propagation Methods

Cultivars of Leichtlin’s camas are widely available in the flower bulb industry. One cultivar, ‘Alba’, is white (or bluish-white), while ‘Caerulea’ has light blue flowers, and ‘Plena’ has double greenish yellow blooms (Brenzel, 1995). ‘Blauwe Donau’ is described as having sea-lavender-violet flowers and lilac anthers. ‘Lady Eve Price’ has campanula-violet flowers with each segment having a darker colored methyl-violet central stripe (Royal General Bulbgrower’s Association, 1991). ‘Semiplena’ has large, semi-double, creamy-white flowers.
