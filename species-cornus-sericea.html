Red-Osier Dogwood

Cornus sericea L. · Cornaceae

Overview

Summary

Upright, multi-stemmed deciduous shrub, 1 to 6 m tall; young branches reddish-green in summer, bright red in winter; simple, opposite ovate leaves turning rich red in fall; greenish-white flowers in flat cymes; white to bluish-white drupe fruits. Deciduous upright shrub, 1 to 3 m high, with spreading rhizomes/prostrate lower branches; deep-red finely hairy twigs with white pith; oval to ovate-lanceolate leaves; flat terminal clusters of white flowers; white round succulent drupe fruit.

Botanical Description

An upright, multi-stemmed deciduous shrub 1 to 6 m (or 1.4-6 m) tall, spreading by rhizomes, prostrate lower branches, or layering of prostrate stems that simulate stolons. Young branches are reddish-green in summer, bright red in winter, maturing to reddish-purple or greyish-green, with deep-red pubescent twigs possessing white pith. Leaves are simple, opposite, oval to ovate-lanceolate, 4-12 cm (or 5-10 cm) long, dark green above and hairy, lighter-colored or whitish below, turning rich red in autumn, featuring smooth margins, rounded bases, pointed tips, and 5-7 prominent parallel or falsely parallel veins converging at the leaf tips with white threads running through them. Inflorescences are flat cymes or terminal clusters of 2-3 mm white to cream-colored flowers blooming from June to August.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabits swamps, thickets, riparian areas, and woodlands, thriving in wet to mesic streamsides, lakesides, swamps, and forests across lowland, steppe, and montane zones. Common throughout British Columbia, ranging north to Alaska, Yukon, and Northwest Territories, east to Newfoundland, and south to West Virginia, Ohio, Nebraska, New Mexico, California, and Mexico. Found along bottomland streambanks and floodplains with fluctuating water tables, nutrient-rich soils, ditches, swamps, and thickets; dry to moist soils (2); the understory of Littlewood Forest (7); moist woods, ponds, riverbanks, thickets, clearings, coulees, and flood plains throughout the Canadian prairies; widespread across Alberta, ranging from Alaska to California, New Mexico, and Ohio (26); and from Ohio to Maine south to Virginia and New Jersey, preferring moist, moderately acidic to neutral medium or coarse soils (43).

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Meadow; Woodland / Shade; Rain Garden / Wetland; Hedgerow / Thicket

Key Features

Pollinator Magnet; Butterfly / Moth Host; Bird Forage; Erosion Control; Edible

Soil Preferences

Redosier dogwood grows in soils that are saturated for at least a portion of the growing season. Not as tolerant of long-term root saturation as are some other shrubs, dogwood seems to prefer wetland margins where soils are nitrogen-rich, saturated, and shallowly inundated in the spring, and may be completely dry by late summer.

Moisture Notes

Thrives in seasonally saturated soils, riparian benches, or poorly drained depressions.

Spread Behaviour

Slowly spreading rhizomatous clump

Root Formation

Rhizomatous

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic but vigorous clonal spreader (Rhizomatous); forms thickets that outcompete slow-establishing forbs—allow generous spacing or use root barriers in tight borders.

Pruning & Management

Redosier dogwood is often coppiced in late fall after the leaves turn brown and fall off the stem. Cut all stems to approximately 2-3 in (5-8 cm) from the base before growth begins in spring. Apply fertilizer around the shrub to promote new growth, then apply mulch around the base. Coppicing stimulates the growth of new, vigorous stems whose deep burgundy color is especially vivid.

Fall & Winter Interest

Bright red winter twigs, brilliant red autumn foliage, and persistent white to bluish winter berries.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Tea from roots treats dizziness. Fruit bitter, eaten fresh/dried/mixed in cakes. Root tea for dizziness.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Tart, bitter white berries eaten in small quantities fresh, dried, or mixed with other fruits.

Material & Technology

Some tribes ate the white, sour berries, while others used the branches for arrow-making, stakes, or other tools. Stems used for spruce bark canoe ribs. Dream catchers, dye, baskets, tanning hides, arrows, tools, toothbrushes.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Tea from stems relieves chest trouble, coughs, fevers, or used as an emetic. Bark decoction as laxative. Peeled bark mixed with other plants treats sore eyes.
