Overview

Summary

A stoloniferous perennial forb with a trailing habit, developing from a fibrous root and short rhizome typically originating from a single crown, accompanied by several leafless runners. Found in meadows and woodlands; inhabiting moist to dry fields, roadsides, meadows, grassy slopes, thickets, forest edges, and open forests in the lowland to subalpine zones; common throughout British Columbia; ranging north to Alaska and Northwest Territories, east to Newfoundland, and south to California, Colorado, Tennessee, and Georgia.

Botanical Description

A stoloniferous perennial forb with a trailing habit, developing from a fibrous root and short rhizome typically originating from a single crown, accompanied by several leafless runners. Perennial herb from a fibrous root and short rhizome usually from a single crown, with several leafless runners; stems trailing, rooting at the nodes; runners, leaf stalks and flower shoots with long spreading to appressed hairs and usually greenish not reddish-tinged. Basal leaves are borne in rosettes, are palmately compound on stalks 2-15 cm long, and consist of 3 short-stalked, egg-shaped leaflets measuring 2-7 cm long. The lower leaf surface ranges from smooth to silky-hairy, while the upper surface is smooth and glaucous bluish-green, with coarsely toothed margins where the terminal tooth is less than 1/2 as wide as and shorter than the adjacent teeth.

Spread Behaviour

Root Formation

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic moderate clonal spreader (Stoloniferous); weaves steadily through mixed groundcover matrices without harsh chemical inhibition.

Pruning & Management

Keep the runners pruned back because they can be invasive. It is necessary to divide the patch every 3 to 4 years and start a new patch for increased vigor. Younger plants are more vigorous and produce more berries.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Fruit edible but not abundant. Midwestern prairie and Great Lakes tribes, such as the Omaha, Hidatsa, Mandan, Dakota, Pawnee, Blackfoot, Cheyenne, and the Winnebago ate these strawberries raw, cooked or dried. Food (fruits & leaves for tea).

Edible Parts & Flavour

Fruits & Leaves (Both Tea Intended); Edible berries and leaves (intended for tea).

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Whole plant, leaves, or roots boiled as decoction to treat heart conditions. Roots, leaves, and runners boiled to treat diarrhoea and dysentery. Dried strawberry leaf tea used as a wash for sores, eczema, and skin problems.

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

This strawberry is best established at higher elevations where a good frost occurs (over 600 m). Dig up plantlets or runners and plant them in pots in fall--being sure to cover the stems and roots in soil.

Seeding Rate (seeds/m²)
