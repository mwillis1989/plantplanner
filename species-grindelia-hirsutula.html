Hairy Gumweed

Overview

Summary

A tufted, sun-loving, drought-tolerant, deer-resistant, and pollinator-friendly perennial forb suited for well-drained rocky sites, reaching 20-60 cm (or 20-80 cm) in height and bearing cheerful yellow flowers. Inhabiting dry rock outcrops and meadows within the lowland zone, this species is rare on the Gulf Islands—known only from Lasqueti Island—and is disjunct south to California.

Botanical Description

Growing from a taproot and a stout, branched stem-base, it produces erect stems that are few-branched above, densely glandular, and interspersed with stiff, translucent hairs; basal leaves are toothed, while stem leaves are alternate, oblong to lanceolate, stiff-hairy, and toothed with regular teeth 1-2.5 mm long. Tufted perennial forb (20–60 cm tall) with sticky gummy heads and bright yellow flowers. Perennial herb from a taproot and a stout, branched stem-base; stems erect, few branched above, densely glandular, also with some stiff, translucent hairs; 20-80 cm tall.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabiting dry rock outcrops and meadows within the lowland zone, this species is rare on the Gulf Islands—known only from Lasqueti Island—and is disjunct south to California. This plant may be found in sandy or saline bottomlands, dry banks, rocky fields, plains, and roadsides (Munz, 1968). The plant may also be found to the east, across the North Coast Ranges and around the San Francisco Bay, north to the Cascade Range foothills, west along the Sierra Nevadas, and south along the outer Southern Coast Ranges into Baja California (Moore, 2012). Great Valley gumweed is native to central California.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Steeped for respiratory ailments. Native Americans regarded Great Valley gumweed as a useful medicinal plant for a variety of treatments, including respiratory and dermatological afflictions. The Costanoan Indians boiled leaves and flower heads of gumweed for healing dermatitis caused by poison oak, and for wounds, burns, boils, and sores.

Safety

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Recorded insect/host associations (beees sheet): Argyrotaenia franciscana: in tied flowers/leaves, on buds; Carmenta texana: in roots/stems; Cremastobombycia grindeliella: in leaves; Oidaematophorus occidentalis: on flowers; Platyptilia williamsii: in tips

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

Sow in fall or early spring for germination in the same season. Propagation is easiest from seed, and germination rates are generally high. Achenes placed under a thin layer of soil on wet filter paper at ambient temperature and diffuse light germinate at around 70%, but an 8-day cool pretreatment at 8°C can raise these rates slightly (McLaughlin, 1987).

Propagation Methods

Sow seeds in fall or early spring. There are no developed cultivars of Great Valley gumweed, but seed is generally available from commercial sources both individually and in pollinator mixes.
