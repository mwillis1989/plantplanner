Cow Parsnip

Heracleum maximum W. Bartram. · Apiaceae

Overview

Summary

Large robust hairy perennial 1-2 m tall with hollow single stem from stout roots; strong pungent odor; large compound leaves up to 40 cm across (immature form single-leaved); flat umbel of small white flowers; heart-shaped winged seeds. Large Perennial Forb, 1-2 m height with a large umbel of small white flowers, hollow stems, and solid leaf stalks.

Botanical Description

Large, striking plant with a large umbel of small white flowers that pollinators enjoy in both full sun and partial shade. Heracleum maximum, commonly known as cow parsnip or hogweed, is a member of the carrot family (Apiaceae). It is a large, striking, biennial or short-lived perennial herb native to North America, growing 1-2 m (or 1-3 m / 0.9–3 m) tall from a fleshy taproot or stout cluster of fibrous roots. Stems are single, hollow, deeply ridged, light green, moderately to very pubescent (lanate or woolly) with spreading white hairs (trichomes), and produce a strong, pungent odor.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabits meadows, thickets, and woodlands, as well as wet to moist areas from the lowland to the alpine zone. It is common throughout British Columbia, exhibiting an amphiberingian distribution extending east to Newfoundland and south to Georgia, New Mexico, and California, with occurrences also in East Asia. Habitats include stream banks, moist slopes, clearings, marshes, meadows, thickets, roadsides, and shaded riparian zones. Cow parsnip grows in partially shaded, wet to somewhat moist environments including forest edges, openings, floodplains, wet meadows, thickets, and streambanks (Reznicek et al., 2011).

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Meadow; Woodland / Shade; Rock Garden; Rain Garden / Wetland; Coastal / Bluff

Key Features

Pollinator Magnet; Butterfly / Moth Host; Deer Resistant; Erosion Control; Edible

Soil Preferences

Cow parsnip is found in a variety of moist soils. It often grows in loamy to sandy loam soils but also occurs in clay and gravelly soil. It grows best in partly shady locations but can withstand full sun provided soil conditions are moist and loamy.

Moisture Notes

Prefers evenly moist, well-drained soils without standing winter water. (Wetland status: FAC.)

Spread Behaviour

Deep-rooted non-spreading clump

Root Formation

Taproot

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic and non-aggressive (Taproot); low root competition makes it safe for intimate mixed borders and diverse polyculture interplanting.

Erosion & Slope Control

Erosion Control: The fleshy roots of cow parsnip can be used for erosion control in areas that are gently sloped (Dittberner & Olson, 1983). It establishes quickly by seed and readily reseeds, making it a good choice for short and long-term revegetation projects (Esser, 1995; Dittberner & Olson, 1983).

Pruning & Management

Cow parsnip needs little care. If plants begin to grow in areas where it is not wanted, it is removable by hand.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Young (peeled) stems and leaves edible. Strongly flavored root edible. Emerging leaf and flower buds were cooked and used in stews.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Young stalks/leaf stems (ya:la') peeled and eaten raw/cooked ('native rhubarb') (caution: juice causes UV-amplified skin blistering); Hollow stems and leaf stalks (ya:la') peeled and eaten (caution: juice causes skin blistering).

Material & Technology

The roots were used to dye fibers yellow. Root yellow dye. Americans Indians used cow parsnip for food, medicine, and tools.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Cow parsnip is still used for food and traditional medicine by American Indians. Leaves, stems, and roots were used in teas, poultices, and powders to treat boils, warts, swelling, and bruising. Ground into a poultice for bruises and cuts.

Safety

Edibility

Edible with Caution

Pets, Children & Livestock

Skin-blistering sap hazard for children and pets brushing foliage in sunlight.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Sap contains furanocoumarins causing severe phytophotodermatitis blisters on sun-exposed skin; wear gloves and wash exposed skin promptly. Peeling outer skin required before cooking young stems. If skin is exposed to the sap of cow parsnip, wash the area with soap and water immediately (NYDEC, n.d.) | Recorded insect/host associations (beees sheet): Eupithecia tripunctaria: on umbels

Propagation & Seeds

Seed Collection

Cow parsnip seed is hand-collected when the fruit turns brown. Dried seed is estimated to be viable for up to three years when stored at 1–3°C (Luna et al., 2008).

Stratification & Dormancy

The water should be changed daily and followed by a 100-day cold-moist stratification period (Luna et al., 2008; moisture should be maintained), before undergoing a second 100-day cold-moist stratification; germination will not take place between the first and second cold moist stratification period. Cow parsnip will not germinate unless its double dormancy is broken.

Sowing Protocol

Cow parsnip can be broadcast or drilled at 1.1–2.2 kg/ha in the fall (Esser, 1995; Horton, 1994). Sow in fall for spring germination.

Propagation Methods

There are currently no named or improved cultivars.
