Overview

Summary

An annual forb growing 40-80 cm in height with yellow flowers and a fragrance resembling tea tree oil mixed with grapefruit, frequently inhabiting clay soils on bare ground between perennials with seasonal inundation and summer drying. Inhabiting meadows, moist to dry roadsides, open slopes, and forest openings in lowland and montane zones, mountain tarweed occurs infrequently and is scattered throughout southern British Columbia, extending east to Saskatchewan and south to Colorado, Arizona, and California.

Botanical Description

This annual often grows in clay soils on the bare ground between perennial plants. It is well-adapted to having water sit on the surface in the winter and drying completely in the summer. It has a fragrance that has been described as tea tree oil mixed with grapefruit. Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabiting meadows, moist to dry roadsides, open slopes, and forest openings in lowland and montane zones, mountain tarweed occurs infrequently and is scattered throughout southern British Columbia, extending east to Saskatchewan and south to Colorado, Arizona, and California. As the most widely distributed Madia species, its native range spans western and northern North America from British Columbia to the Southwestern United States, across southwestern Canada, and into the Rocky Mountains, with naturalized populations in Alaska, eastern Canada, and the north-central and northeastern United States. It predominantly occupies disturbed sites—including grassland openings, meadows, swales, shrublands, woodlands, forests, marsh and lake edges, and roadsides—at elevations of 0–3658 m.

Erosion & Slope Control

Restoration: Mountain tarweed can be planted on restoration sites that contain upland areas such as meadows, oak savannas, or shrublands.

Pruning & Management

Control of weedy perennial species that may out-compete mountain tarweed over time may be necessary. Encroachment of trees and shrubs will shade out this species. Although this species is an annual herb, it readily self-sows and persists.

Propagation & Seeds

Stratification & Dormancy

In trials done at the Corvallis Plant Materials Center, mountain tarweed seeds sown in the fall germinated in late winter to early spring, just as temperatures start to increase slightly. Seeds sown in early spring also germinated well, suggesting that cold stratification is not required.

Sowing Protocol

Seed can be broadcast onto sites or planted with a seed drill at a depth of ¼ inch. Seeds can also be sown directly into holes in the weed fabric in early spring.

Propagation Methods

Contact your local Natural Resource Conservation Service office for more information on availability. Field establishment: For best establishment, grow plugs in a greenhouse in late winter and transplant out in spring. Transplant into a field covered with weed fabric containing holes on 0.3 m by 0.3 m spacing.
