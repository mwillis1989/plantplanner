Pacific Ninebark

Physocarpus capitatus · Rosaceae

🌿 NativeBC CDC: YellowShrubZone 4a–8b🦋 3 Lepidoptera hosts

Overview

Summary

Deciduous shrub up to 4 m tall with reddish peeling papery/attractive bark; 3-5 lobed serrated leaves; small white 5-petaled flowers with pink stamens in clusters ('Snowball bush'). Found in riparian areas, thickets, and woodlands, specifically thriving in moist to wet streamside thickets, forest edges, open forests, margins of lakes and marshes, clearings, and roadsides in the lowland to montane zones; frequent in southwestern British Columbia and locally frequent in southeastern British Columbia, ranging north to Alaska and south to northern Idaho and California.

Botanical Description

Medium to tall shrub, 1-4 m tall; branches erect to arching, angled, smooth to minutely star-shaped-hairy when young, eventually with brown, shredding bark. The alternate, deciduous leaves have stalks 1-3 cm long and egg- to heart-shaped blades 3-10 cm long, palmately 3- or 5-lobed with irregularly double-toothed lobes, deeply veined, shiny dark green above, and paler with abundant star-shaped hairs below. Deciduous shrub up to 4 m tall with reddish peeling papery/attractive bark; 3-5 lobed serrated leaves; small white 5-petaled flowers with pink stamens in clusters ('Snowball bush'). Deciduous shrub (up to 4 m tall) with reddish, papery shredding bark peeling in layers, 3- to 5-lobed leaves, and dense rounded clusters ("snowballs") of small white flowers.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Found in riparian areas, thickets, and woodlands, specifically thriving in moist to wet streamside thickets, forest edges, open forests, margins of lakes and marshes, clearings, and roadsides in the lowland to montane zones; frequent in southwestern British Columbia and locally frequent in southeastern British Columbia, ranging north to Alaska and south to northern Idaho and California. It prefers moist soil in partial shade west of the Cascades and occurs across Alaska to southern California across riparian habitats, streams, lake shores, and slopes.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Seed edible.

Material & Technology

Branches used for arrows (Karok, Hesquiat). Bark soaked with cedar bark for dark brown dye. Wood made into knitting needles (Quw'utsun' (Cowichan)).

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Outer bark tea used as laxative, emetic (induce vomiting), and treatment for gonorrhea and sores (Nuxalk, Coast Salish, Hesquiat). Root extract used as laxative (Kwakwaka'wakw (Kwakiutl), W̱SÁNEĆ (Saanich)). Toxic bark tea used to induce vomiting, or as laxative/wash for sores.
