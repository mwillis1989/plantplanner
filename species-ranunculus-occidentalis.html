Overview

Summary

A perennial forb and native member of the Ranunculaceae family, standing 15-50 cm tall (or stems measuring 15-40 (60) cm tall, extending from 15.2 cm to 0.8 m tall) with yellow flowers bearing 5 to 14 petals. Found in meadows and woodlands, specifically occupying moist to mesic meadows, grassy slopes, coastal bluffs, shores, clearings, and open forests from lowland to subalpine zones; common in coastal and northern British Columbia, infrequent elsewhere, ranging north to Alaska, Yukon, and Northwest Territories, east to Alberta, and south to California.

Botanical Description

A perennial forb and native member of the Ranunculaceae family, standing 15-50 cm tall (or stems measuring 15-40 (60) cm tall, extending from 15.2 cm to 0.8 m tall) with yellow flowers bearing 5 to 14 petals. Perennial herb from a cluster of fibrous roots; stems 1 to several, erect or nearly so, 15-40 (60) cm tall, more or less hollow; plants variously hairy. Basal leaves are broadly egg-shaped, semi-circular, or kidney-shaped, measuring ¾ to 5.1 cm long by ¾ to 7.6 cm wide (specifically blades 1.5-5.5 cm long, 2-8 cm wide), deeply 3-lobed or -parted, with segments 1- to 2-times incised and ultimate segments oblong, elliptic, oblanceolate, or lanceolate, toothed, and tips pointed to rounded; leaf stalks are long, measuring 1 to several times the blade length. Stem leaves are alternate, fewer, stay low to the base, are more deeply dissected, shorter-stalked, and transition to 3- to 7-parted lower bracts and entire upper bracts.

Name Etymology

Genus Ranunculus; species Ranunculus occidentalis.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Found in meadows and woodlands, specifically occupying moist to mesic meadows, grassy slopes, coastal bluffs, shores, clearings, and open forests from lowland to subalpine zones; common in coastal and northern British Columbia, infrequent elsewhere, ranging north to Alaska, Yukon, and Northwest Territories, east to Alberta, and south to California. This species prefers seasonally moist soils across diverse habitats ranging from coastal prairies and bluffs to disturbed areas, meadows, and forested zones (Turner and Gustafson, 2006), generally associating with wetlands while tolerating seasonal drought and inhabiting non-wetland sites. It occurs from sea level to 2134 m elevations in western North America, extending from California and western Nevada north through western Canada and into Alaska. It is generally associated with wetland areas, although it will tolerate seasonal drought and is also found in non-wetland sites.

Soil Preferences

While western buttercup prefers vernal pools and wetland sites, it will also tolerate drier upland sites away from the coast that retain seasonally moist soils. It also does quite well in plant and seed production fields, tolerating all soil types and a range of pH values.

Moisture Notes

Adapted to well-drained winter-wet soils with prolonged summer dry periods.

Spread Behaviour

Mycorrhizal Association

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic and non-aggressive (Fibrous); low root competition makes it safe for intimate mixed borders and diverse polyculture interplanting.

Erosion & Slope Control

Restoration: This plant is a great choice for wetland prairie restoration throughout its range. Its versatility enables it to thrive in moist upland or lowland sites as a quickly establishing native (Stanley et al., 2010).

Pruning & Management

Once established in an appropriate environment, this species is relatively low-maintenance. On a restoration site, it is not aggressive and unlikely to dominate and become weedy like some other Ranunculus species. It can tolerate herbicide application during its late summer dormancy, and would likely benefit from mowing in late summer to reduce non-native competition on a restoration site.

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

This is a hardy plant with few, if any, susceptibilities to disease or pests. The fresh leaves, stems, and flower sap of other members of the Ranunculus genus have been found to cause digestive or nervous system problems for livestock grazing in quantity.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

This is a hardy plant with few, if any, susceptibilities to disease or pests. The fresh leaves, stems, and flower sap of other members of the Ranunculus genus have been found to cause digestive or nervous system problems for livestock grazing in buttercup-dominated areas. Fresh leaves, stems, and flower sap contain protoanemonin and are toxic if ingested raw; the Aleut noted that juices of its blooms are poisonous.

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

Broadcast at a single species application rate of 6 to 7 pounds pure live seed (pls) per acre. Western buttercup has approximately 200,000 seeds per pound, so applying seed at a rate of one pound per acre will result in about 5 seeds per square foot. Field Establishment & Management: There are limited labeled herbicides appropriate for forb species in seed production; therefore, a weed free seedbed is very important. Germination begins 5 to 6 weeks after fall sowing. At the Corvallis PMC, fields are drill planted at a rate of 6 to 7 lb pls/acre at a depth of 3–6 mm with row spacing of 25.4–35.6 cm. When establishing a field with transplants, plugs can be planted as close as 0.3 m centers as plants do not take up much space, but row spacing should be planned to accommodate cultivation and harvest equipment.

Propagation Methods

There are no improved cultivars of western buttercup, but seed is sometimes available from commercial growers in the Pacific Northwest.
