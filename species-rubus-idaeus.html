Red Raspberry

Overview

Summary

Erect perennial shrub 1 to 2 m high; biennial upright spiny/bristly canes with shredding bark; 1st year leaves pinnate (3-5 leaflets), 2nd year trifoliate; white drooping flowers in cymes; red aggregate ovoid fruit (1 cm). Occurs in mesic to moist thickets, rocky slopes, clearings, burns, old fields, and open forests in the lowland and montane zones; common throughout British Columbia, mostly in and east of the Coast-Cascade Mountains; ranging east to Newfoundland and south to California, northern Mexico, and North Carolina.

Botanical Description

Medium shrub, 0.5-2 m tall, perennial with biennial stems (canes); stems erect to ascending, almost unarmed to prickly and bristly, often glandular-hairy, sometimes smooth and glaucous beneath the prickles; bark yellow to cinnamon-brown, shredding; similar to cultivated raspberry. Leaves alternate, deciduous, pinnately compound, 5-20 cm long; leaflets 3 to 5 on first-year canes, mostly 3 on flowering canes, egg-shaped to broadly lanceolate, 3-10 cm long, double-saw-toothed and sharply long-pointed at the tip, greenish and smooth to sparsely hairy above, paler and greyish-woolly to nearly smooth beneath, the veins and leaf-stalks often glandular-prickly; stipules linear-awl-shaped, 4-10 mm long. Blossoms are white to greenish white, drooping, single or in small grapelike clusters.

Name Etymology

Genus Rubus; species Rubus idaeus.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Occurs in mesic to moist thickets, rocky slopes, clearings, burns, old fields, and open forests in the lowland and montane zones; common throughout British Columbia, mostly in and east of the Coast-Cascade Mountains; ranging east to Newfoundland and south to California, northern Mexico, and North Carolina. It occupies open woods, thickets, clearings, burned areas, riverbanks, roadsides, and trails on fine to coarse soils; it is drought-tolerant, adapted to nutrient-rich conditions, and tolerates a wide pH range. A circumpolar species distributed from Alberta across Canada, the United States, and Eurasia, the American red raspberry is native to North America and also grows from northern Europe to northwestern Asia.

Garden Uses

Key Features

Soil Preferences

This species grows good in well-drained loamy soil in a sunny location or in a semi-shaded area. It tolerates a wide range of soil pH texture and requires adequate soil moisture.

Moisture Notes

Adapted to well-drained winter-wet soils with prolonged summer dry periods.

Spread Behaviour

Fibrous-rooted clump

Root Formation

Fibrous

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Strong clonal colony competitor (Fibrous); moderate allelopathic/litter suppression combined with dense rhizome or sucker expansion—give clear spacing from delicate spring ephemerals.

Erosion & Slope Control

Invades plots rapidly, declines over time; forms thickets for erosion control and soil stability; common invader of disturbed sites (amended tailings, roadsides); grows on infertile bare mineral soil; susceptible to sodium/sulfate tailings water.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Fresh and canned fruit eaten or made into syrups. Young stems peeled and eaten. Older stems and roots boiled to make a beverage.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Fresh/canned fruit eaten, syrups; young stems peeled and eaten; older stems/roots boiled for beverage; berry/leaf tea for morning sickness; leaf facial tonic.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Stems used to relieve fevers, childhood diarrhoea, stomach aches, dysentery, cholera, teething, help with recovery from childbirth, and used in eye drop solutions. Roots and stems used as a wash for infections. Roots used in asthma treatment.
