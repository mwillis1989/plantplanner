Wapato

Sagittaria latifolia Willd. · Alismataceae

Overview

Summary

Herbaceous aquatic perennial geophyte, 20-90 cm tall with distinctive waxy arrowhead-shaped leaves and starchy submerged tubers/bulbs. Inhabiting wet ditches, ponds, lakeshores, and marshes across lowland, steppe, and montane zones; infrequent in British Columbia south of 56°N, absent from the Queen Charlotte Islands, northern Vancouver Island, and the adjacent coast; extending east to Quebec, New Brunswick, and Nova Scotia, and south to Maine, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, Florida, Mississippi, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, and California; also found in northern South America and Hawaii.

Botanical Description

An herbaceous aquatic perennial geophyte, 20-90 cm tall, characterized by waxy arrowhead-shaped leaves and starchy submerged tubers/bulbs. It arises as a perennial aquatic or semi-aquatic herb from a tuber-producing rhizome with stems 20-50 (90) cm tall. All leaves are basal; emergent leaf blades are arrowhead-shaped, 5-25 cm long, long-stalked and sheathed at the base, and submerged leaf blades are lanceolate or linear and essentially bladeless, up to 80 cm long. Representing the Arrowhead Family (Alismataceae), both Sagittaria latifolia and Sagittaria cuneata grow in swampy ground or standing water in ponds, lakes, stream edges, and ditches (Hickman 1993), producing edible white or bluish tubers.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabiting wet ditches, ponds, lakeshores, and marshes across lowland, steppe, and montane zones; infrequent in British Columbia south of 56°N, absent from the Queen Charlotte Islands, northern Vancouver Island, and the adjacent coast; extending east to Quebec, New Brunswick, and Nova Scotia, and south to Maine, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, Florida, Mississippi, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, and California; also found in northern South America and Hawaii. Occurs in wetlands, swamps, lowlands, marshes, estuaries, and ponds.

Successional Role

Early to mid-seral wetland/aquatic plant. Wetland aquatic pioneer.

Fire Ecology

We have no record of specific traditional resource management techniques other than anecdotal information of the use of fire to keep dense tule marshes open, which provided an opportunity for colonization and spread of Sagittaria species.

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Meadow; Rain Garden / Wetland; Hedgerow / Thicket

Key Features

Edible

Soil Preferences

Wetland margins, river deltas, marshes, shallow lakes, sloughs, and mud flats. Submerged in shallow waters. Saturated, muddy, or submerged organic aquatic soils.

Moisture Notes

Thrives in seasonally saturated soils, riparian benches, or poorly drained depressions.

Spread Behaviour

Slowly multiplying clump from underground bulbs or corms

Root Formation

Bulb / Corm / Tuber

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic and non-aggressive (Bulb / Corm / Tuber); low root competition makes it safe for intimate mixed borders and diverse polyculture interplanting.

Pruning & Management

Hydrology is the most important factor in determining wetland type, revegetation success, and wetland function and value. Changes in water levels influence species composition, structure, and distribution of plant communities. Water management is absolutely critical during plant establishment, and remains crucial through the life of the wetland for proper community management. Sagittaria species require moist soils to standing water for successful revegetation.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Edible bulb. Sagittaria is an aquatic plant with tuberous roots that can be eaten like potatoes. Edible wetland root/tuber vegetable harvested in river bays and deltas.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Starchy tubers roasted, eaten raw, or milled into flour (tasting like potatoes).

Material & Technology

Indian women collected it in shallow water from a canoe, or waded into ponds or marshes in the late summer and loosened the roots with their toes. Tubers harvested from mud in fall/winter by wading or using canoes. The roots would rise to the top of the water where they were gathered and tossed into floating baskets.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Root tea used for digestive tract issues. Root poultice used to heal cuts and infections. Leaf/root teas for lactation, digestion, cuts. Leaf and root teas prepared for lactation, digestion, and cuts.

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Propagation & Seeds

Propagation Methods

Sagittaria species may be planted from bare root stock, by transplanting the tubers, and by seeding directly into wetland soil. Live plant transplants or transplanting tubers are preferred revegetation methods where there is moving water.
