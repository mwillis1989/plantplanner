Overview

Summary

Deciduous Shrub up to 8 m height. Inhabits wetlands and riparian areas, including moist to mesic thickets, floodplains, lakeshores, avalanche tracks, clearings, and forest margins within lowland and montane zones.

Botanical Description

Deciduous Shrub up to 8 m height. Dioecious shrubs or trees, 1-8 m tall, non-colonial; branches erect with flexible bases; twigs yellow- to red-brown and densely hairy. Leaves alternate, simple, elliptic to lance- or egg-shaped, 3-12 cm long, 1.7-5 cm wide; lower surface very densely silky, satiny, or woolly with white hairs; upper surface dull, sparsely long soft-hairy to nearly smooth; margins entire or toothed; bases pointed; tips rounded to pointed. Leaf stalks lack glandular dots at the top; stipules leaflike or rudimentary.

Name Etymology

Genus Salix; geographic specific epithet sitchensis denotes the plant's North American regional distribution.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabits wetlands and riparian areas, including moist to mesic thickets, floodplains, lakeshores, avalanche tracks, clearings, and forest margins within lowland and montane zones. It is common in southern British Columbia south of 56°N, particularly west of the Coast-Cascade Mountains, and infrequent northward; ranging north to southern Alaska, east to Alberta, and south to Montana, Idaho, and California. Salix sitchensis is native to stream banks from southern Alaska to southwestern Oregon (Viereck & Little 1972).

Soil Preferences

Sitka willow is found on or near lake shores, wetland margins, forest edges, wet openings, and clearings at low to middle elevations. This species prefers damp, heavy soil but will succeed in most soils.

Moisture Notes

Thrives in seasonally saturated soils, riparian benches, or poorly drained depressions.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

The inner bark was dried, ground into a powder and then added to cereal for use in making bread.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Documented traditional culinary use.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Willows are the source of the natural precursor to aspirin, salicylic acid, found in leaves and bark. The bark can be pounded and applied to wounds as a healing agent. An infusion of the stems has been used in the treatment of stomach complaints.

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

Seeds are viable for only a few days and the maximum storage period is four to six weeks with germination rates dropping off fast after ten days at room temperature. Willow seeds have no dormancy and germinate within twelve to twenty-four hours after falling on moist ground.

Propagation Methods

Seeds must be surface sown as soon as they are ripe in late spring.
