Botanical Description

A deciduous, erect, slender shrub up to 1-2 m high, rhizomatous, bearing opposite oval wavy leaves, pink and white bell-shaped hairy flowers, and a dry waxy white spherical non-edible berry (6-12 mm) containing 2 seeds. It is a much-branched shrub, often densely colonial from rhizomes, 0.5-3 m tall, with older stems dark greyish-brown with shredding bark, glabrous branchlets, and thin, wiry twigs. Leaves are opposite, on stalks 2-4 mm long, elliptic to oval, 1.5-5 cm long and 1-3.5 cm wide, obtuse at base and tip, with margins entire or occasionally bearing a few wavy teeth; leaves are often larger and irregularly lobed on sterile shoots, and are glabrous above, and glabrous or spreading-hairy beneath.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Occurring in meadows, thickets, wetlands, and woodlands, as well as mesic to dry meadows, disturbed areas, grasslands, shrublands, and forests across the lowland, steppe, and montane zones. Extremely common in southern British Columbia, common in central British Columbia, and infrequent northward; extending north to the Northwest Territories, east to Quebec, and south to Nebraska, Virginia, Colorado, and California. Habitat includes bushy areas, open woodland, valley slopes, grasslands, ravines, and disturbed ground in rocky or coarse soils under wet to dry conditions, mild acid to alkaline pH, and moderate salinity (Alberta, southeastern Alaska to Quebec, south to California, Virginia). Snowberry inhabits slopes and valley bottoms of the foothills of the Coast Ranges, the Sierra Nevada, and the mountains of southern California (McMinn 1939), extending northward to British Columbia and eastward to Pennsylvania and the New England states.

Soil Preferences

Symphoricarpos albus is found along stream banks, in swampy thickets, moist clearings and open forests at sea level to middle elevations MacKinnon 1994. It tolerates soil types but grows best in heavy clay soils.

Moisture Notes

Prefers evenly moist, well-drained soils without standing winter water.

Spread Behaviour

Rhizomatous colony- or thicket-former

Root Formation

Rhizomatous

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Strong clonal colony competitor (Rhizomatous); moderate allelopathic/litter suppression combined with dense rhizome or sucker expansion—give clear spacing from delicate spring ephemerals.

Erosion & Slope Control

Slope & bank stabilization.

Fall & Winter Interest

Persistent fruit, hips, cones, or dried seedheads add late-season visual interest and winter wildlife forage.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Edible Parts & Flavour

Inedible / Toxic.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

One or two of the berries were eaten by the Stl’atl’imx to settle the stomach after too much fatty food. An infusion of the fruit was used as eyewash for sore eyes and the berries were rubbed on the skin as treatment for burns, rashes, and sores. Fruits crushed or boiled to make a wash for sore eyes. A tea made from the roots of this species was used to clear up afterbirth. Wash used for skin rashes or mixed with other plants for venereal disease tea.

Safety

Edibility

No / Toxic

Pets, Children & Livestock

Keep showy fruits out of reach of young children; mildly irritating if ingested in quantity.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Saponin is much more toxic to some creatures, such as fish, if eaten in large quantities. Snowberry fruit contains low concentrations of a bitter principle, saponin, which foams in water. It is very poorly absorbed by the body and can be broken down by thoroughly cooking the fruit. | Recorded insect/host associations (beees sheet): Argyrotaenia franciscana: in tied flowers/leaves; Euceratia castella: on buds/flowers/leaves; Onceroptila cygnodiella: in leaves; Phyllonorycter emberizaepenella: in leaves; Phyllonorycter sp.: in leaves; Sphinx perelegans: on leaves

Propagation & Seeds

Stratification & Dormancy

Dormancy of this species is caused by hard seed coat and immature embryo, which can be broken by stratification in sand and peat for 90 days at 25°C, plus 180 days at 5°C.

Sowing Protocol

Symphoricarpos albus seeds are best sown in the fall after maturity. When the seedlings are large enough to handle, place them into individual pots and grow them in the greenhouse for their first winter.

Propagation Methods

Propagated by seed, root suckers, division, or hardwood cuttings.
