Western Hemlock

Tsuga heterophylla (Raf.) Sarg. · Pinaceae

🌿 NativeBC CDC: YellowTreeZone 4a–8b🦋 115 Lepidoptera hosts

Overview

Summary

Large coniferous tree with shallow roots, short feathery needles, very small cones, and flexible wood. Inhabits moist to dry slopes, river terraces, and flats in lowland and montane zones; common in and west of the Coast-Cascade Mountains in western British Columbia and in southeastern British Columbia; ranging north to southeastern Alaska and south to northern California, northern Idaho, and northwestern Montana.

Botanical Description

A large coniferous tree reaching up to 50 (60) m tall, characterized by shallow roots, flexible wood, a narrow crown with a noticeably drooping leader, and young twigs covered with a mix of short and longer hairs. The bark is rough, scaly, and strongly furrowed and ridged in older trees, transitioning from smooth and reddish-brown when young to darker brown or reddish-brown. Needles are somewhat flattened, rounded at the tip, 8-20 (25) mm long, characteristically unequal in length, short-stalked, finely toothed, irregularly spaced, and spread unevenly at right angles to form feathery, flat sprays; they are glossy yellow-green above and almost entirely white-stomatiferous below. Seed cones mature from green to brown, are ovoid to oblong-egg-shaped, 1.5-2.5 cm long, stalkless, and hang down at the end of the twigs with many thin papery scales.

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabits moist to dry slopes, river terraces, and flats in lowland and montane zones; common in and west of the Coast-Cascade Mountains in western British Columbia and in southeastern British Columbia; ranging north to southeastern Alaska and south to northern California, northern Idaho, and northwestern Montana. In coastal British Columbia, it inhabits the understory of mature forests on old stumps and nurse logs. Western hemlock is native in northwestern California, Oregon, Washington, British Columbia, Alaska, Idaho, and Montana (McMinn & Maino 1963). In California, western hemlock occurs near the coast in scattered localities from Del Norte County southward to the vicinity of Elk Creek, Mendocino County.

Successional Role

In slopefoot situations with organic soils (humisols), Thuja plicata and Tsuga heterophylla eventually dominate as late-successional climax canopy; transition stands are dominated by Thuja plicata and Tsuga heterophylla with minor Pinus.

Soil Preferences

Western hemlock occurs on a variety of soil types. This plant is well adapted to grow on humus and decaying wood, and is also found on mineral soil MacKinnon 1994.

Moisture Notes

Thrives in seasonally saturated soils, riparian benches, or poorly drained depressions.

Spread Behaviour

Woody crown and lateral root system

Root Formation

Deep lateral / Taproot / Woody burl

Mycorrhizal Association

Ectomycorrhizal (EcM; Suillus, Russula, Lactarius, Amanita & forest truffle symbionts)

Competition / Allelopathy

Acidic needle duff, dense feeder-root competition, and year-round canopy shade out sun-loving meadow herbs; underplant with adapted acidophilic woodland understory flora.

Pruning & Management

Seedlings transplant well when they are 20.3–91.4 cm tall. The best survival rate can be achieved if seedlings are transplanted between 8 and 50.8 cm, this is usually when they are about five to eight years old. Transplanting larger seedlings will check badly and hardly put on any growth for several years. This slow growth predisposes the seedling to pest, poor root development and wind throws, and related damage.

Fall & Winter Interest

Year-round evergreen foliage anchors winter garden structure.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

New leaves useful as tea. Large boughs tied in bundles and submerged to collect spawning herring eggs for food. Dried cambium inner bark eaten fresh or dried into square cakes.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Boughs bundled and submerged to collect herring spawn (adds mild flavour); wood steamed for fish hooks.

Material & Technology

Bark used for reddish-brown dye. Western hemlock bark has high tannin content and was used as a tanning agent, pigment and cleansing solution. Some Coast Salish people used a red dye made from hemlock bark to color mountain goat wool and basket materials and as a facial cosmetic and hair remover.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Western hemlock was commonly employed medicinally by several native North American Indian tribes who used it to treat a variety of complaints. Hemlock pitch was applied for a variety of purposes, including poultices or poultice coverings, linaments rubbed on the chest for colds and when mixed with deer tallow as a salve to prevent sunburn. A decoction of the pounded bark has been used in the treatment of hemorrhages, diaphoretic, and diuretic.

Propagation & Seeds

Stratification & Dormancy

Seed is best sown in a cold frame in the autumn or late winter. Dormancy is variable with some seed lots requiring cold stratification (Dirr & Heuser 1987). Stratification accelerates and improves total germination and, unless seeds are known not to require pretreatment, cold stratification at 5°C from three weeks to three months is recommended.
