Cattail

Typha latifolia L. · Typhaceae

🌿 NativeBC CDC: YellowGrass / Sedge / RushZone 4a–8b🦋 22 Lepidoptera hosts

Overview

Summary

Tall Rhizomatous Semiaquatic Perennial up to 1-3 m height; flat grayish-green sheathing leaves (1-2 cm wide); dark brown cylindrical fluffy flower spike (female below, male above). Inhabits lakeshores, wetlands, wet ditches, ponds, and marshes across lowland, steppe, and montane zones; common in southern British Columbia, less frequent in northeastern British Columbia; circumpolar, ranging north to Alaska, Yukon Territory, and Northwest Territories, east to Newfoundland, and south to Maine, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, Florida, Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, Mexico, and California; also found in Eurasia and North Africa from sea level to mid-elevations.

Botanical Description

A tall, rhizomatous semiaquatic perennial herb of wetlands, ponds, and lakes, growing 100-300 cm tall (1-3 m height, 15-30 dm tall) from tough, extensive rhizomes with hard, pithy, erect, simple stems. All parts of the plant are edible*; if using this plant in that way, be careful that it is grown away from contaminants.

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Meadow; Rain Garden / Wetland

Key Features

Bird Forage; Deer Resistant; Winter Interest; Edible

Soil Preferences

Typha latifolia has been found to be tolerant of water level fluctuations and moderate soil salinity.

Moisture Notes

Thrives in seasonally saturated soils, riparian benches, or poorly drained depressions.

Spread Behaviour

Slowly spreading rhizomatous clump

Root Formation

Rhizomatous

Mycorrhizal Association

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM; Glomeromycota endomycorrhizal network)

Competition / Allelopathy

Strong clonal colony competitor (Rhizomatous); moderate allelopathic/litter suppression combined with dense rhizome or sucker expansion—give clear spacing from delicate spring ephemerals.

Erosion & Slope Control

Bioremediator (absorbs pollutants).

Pruning & Management

Heavy grazing will eliminate Typha species as well as other native species from riparian corridors. Cattails are fairly resistant to moderate grazing, providing wet soils are not compacted. Field trials demonstrate that mowing cattails after the heads are well formed but not mature and then following up with another mowing about a month later, when new growth is two or three feet high, will kill at least 75% of the plants.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

All parts edible except for mature leaves/stems/flowers. All parts of the cattail are edible when gathered at the appropriate stage of growth. The young shoots are cut from the rhizomes (underground stems) in the spring when they are about 10.2–40.6 cm long.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Edible (all parts): young shoots, roasted base, steamed flower heads, pollen flour, starch-rich rhizomes.

Material & Technology

Leaves woven into flexible baskets and mats for sleeping, sitting, working, doorways, and shade (Klamath, Modoc). Leaves/sheaths used as caulking to seal joints. Flower fluff/down used for tinder, bedding, insulation, and cradleboard lining.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Base jelly or ground rhizome applied directly or in a poultice to soothe wounds and pain. Wound jelly from stem. Gel from leaf bases or rhizome poultice applied to wounds and to soothe pain.

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Recorded insect/host associations (beees sheet): Limnaecia phragmitella: in heads, in seed heads; Bactra maiorina: on heads; Dicymolomia julianalis: on heads; Acrocercops tricuneatella: in leaves; Calamotropha lupatus: in stems; Calamotropha paludella: in stems

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

Sow in fall for spring germination. Cold stratification required.

Propagation Methods

Local sources are recommended.
