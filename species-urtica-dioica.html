Stinging Nettle

Urtica dioica (Wats.) Hitchc. · Urticaceae

🌿 NativeBC CDC: YellowWildflower (Forb)Zone 4a–9b🦋 50 Lepidoptera hosts

Overview

Summary

A tall, vigorous rhizomatous perennial forb growing 1 to 2 m height (up to 1.5 m height) with fibrous stems, covered in stinging hairs containing irritating chemicals that protect the nutritious leaves from herbivores. Inhabits rich forests, meadows, woodlands, floodplains, wetlands, riverbanks, middens, and old settlements across a soil pH range of 5-8.8, showing strong salinity tolerance.

Botanical Description

Tall, vigorous, perennial that is an important host plant for several native butterflies with stinging hairs that protect the nutritious leaves from herbivores. Inhabits rich forests, meadows, woodlands, floodplains, wetlands, riverbanks, middens, and old settlements across a soil pH range of 5-8.8, showing strong salinity tolerance.

Garden Design & Growing

Garden Uses

Meadow; Woodland / Shade

Key Features

Butterfly / Moth Host; Deer Resistant; Edible

Moisture Notes

Prefers evenly moist, well-drained soils without standing winter water.

Spread Behaviour

Rhizomatous colony- or thicket-former

Root Formation

Rhizomatous

Mycorrhizal Association

Typically Non-Mycorrhizal or facultatively weakly AM (specialized root hairs or wetland nutrient uptake)

Competition / Allelopathy

Non-allelopathic but vigorous clonal spreader (Rhizomatous); forms thickets that outcompete slow-establishing forbs—allow generous spacing or use root barriers in tight borders.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Food (leaves for pesto, salad & soup). Young spring sprouts blanched/cooked and eaten like spinach (rich in iron, Vit C). Young sprouts eaten in springtime.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Leaves (Pesto, Salad & Soup Intended); young spring sprouts blanched/cooked and eaten like spinach (rich in iron, Vit C); Edible leaves intended for pesto, salad & soup.

Material & Technology

Stem fibers processed/twisted into thin strong lines for fishing, hunting nets, cordage, weaving. Cordage/fiber source (nets, string), medicine (counter-irritant for rash). Traditionally utilized for fishing twine and spring greens.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Medicine for arthritis, colds, asthma. Stem fibres twisted and wound to make thin strong fishing line. Roots used as arthritis medicine.

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Recorded insect/host associations (beees sheet): Aglais milberti: on leaves; Pandemis lamprosana: on leaves; Vanessa annabella: on leaves; Vanessa atalanta: on leaves; Abrostola trigemina: on leaves; Abrostola triplasia: on leaves

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

Stinging Nettle seeds need light to germinate, so cover seeds lightly with soil if at all. After sowing, the small seeds will wash into the soil profile. Sow in fall for spring germination.
