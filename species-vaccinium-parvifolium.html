Red Huckleberry

Overview

Summary

Shrub growing up to 4 m tall; produces small bright red, plump, sweet-tart berries (up to 1 cm). Inhabits dry to moist forests and decaying wood across lowland and montane zones; common in western British Columbia, mainly west of the Coast-Cascade Mountains, and infrequent in southeastern British Columbia.

Botanical Description

Red huckleberry is an erect deciduous shrub (Ericaceae) growing 1-4 m tall (up to 4 m tall), featuring bright green, very prominently angled stems that are glabrous or minutely hairy when young, with strongly angled twigs. Leaves are alternate, deciduous (though often some are persistent), oval to oblong-elliptic (elliptic to ovate), 0.9-3 cm long, 0.4-1.6 cm wide, thin, with entire margins (the juvenile leaves persistent, dark green, and finely toothed), glabrous to minutely hairy, and tips usually rounded (with a sharp point at the tip). Flowers are greenish to pinkish, with blossoms solitary in the axils of the lowest leaves of the youngest shoots. Shrub growing up to 4 m tall; produces small bright red, plump, sweet-tart berries (up to 1 cm).

Habitat & BC Range

Natural Habitat

Inhabits dry to moist forests and decaying wood across lowland and montane zones; common in western British Columbia, mainly west of the Coast-Cascade Mountains, and infrequent in southeastern British Columbia. Its distribution extends northward to southeastern Alaska and southward to California, including Saltspring, Matia, Decatur, Cypress, Patos, and Lopez Islands, with common occurrence in Oregon, Washington, and British Columbia. It occurs across a range of elevations in moist to dry forests, open woodlands, and on rotten logs, frequently growing on old stumps and within the shrub layer of Littlewood Forest. Red huckleberry typically grows on old decaying stumps or logs in moist coniferous woods, wetlands, or wetland transition zones, being absent or scarce in dry woods. It does best in full or partial shade.

It requires excessive drainage and acidic soils to become established.

Thrives in seasonally saturated soils, riparian benches, or poorly drained depressions.

Mycorrhizal Association

Ericoid Mycorrhiza (ErM; Rhizoscyphus ericae & acidophilic humus fungi)

Competition / Allelopathy

Forms tight ericaceous root mats and phenolic leaf litter that gradually acidifies surface humus and slows annual weed invasion; compatible with acid-tolerant woodland companions.

Erosion & Slope Control

Grows from Douglas fir logging stumps.

Pruning & Management

This plant grows very rapidly in moist, shady conditions. If summer drought occurs, the plants should be watered so roots are kept fairly moist. For the Hesquiat of Vancouver Island, stumps of old conifers become tended gardens for red huckleberry and salal berry orchards; branches are pruned after berry harvest to stimulate new growth and fruit production.

Fall & Winter Interest

Persistent fruit, hips, cones, or dried seedheads add late-season visual interest and winter wildlife forage.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

Picked and harvested for its edible fruit in summertime. Food (fruits). Berries harvested by striking branches (sqw’uqw) over spread cloth and eaten fresh.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Fruits; Berries eaten fresh, cooked, baked into jam/pie; juice stimulates appetite.

Material & Technology

Gathered by knocking branches over mats.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Bark and leaf infusion gargled for sore throat. Medicine.

Safety

Edibility

Yes

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

Recorded insect/host associations (beees sheet): Acrobasis vaccinii: in fruits, on; Acleris curvalana: on leaves; Actebia fennica: on leaves; Caloptilia anthobaphes: on; Lotisma trigonana: on leaves/flowers/fruits

Propagation & Seeds

Seed Collection

Collect fruit when berries are ripe (they should be bright red). Fruit ripens from July to August and is easily collected by hand picking or by beating the bush over a large bucket. Following collection, chill the fruit to 10°C for several days.

Propagation Methods

Red huckleberry is best grown from seed or by layering.
