Overview

Summary

Occurring in dry to moist open woods, meadows, and roadsides, this species is common throughout British Columbia across all vegetation zones, extending north to southern Alaska, the Yukon Territory, and the Northwest Territories, east to Quebec and Nova Scotia, and south to Maine, New York, Michigan, Minnesota, Iowa, South Dakota, New Mexico, Utah, Nevada, and California. Hookedspur violet inhabits varied environments including dry to moist meadows, coastal areas, open woods, grasslands, and open, disturbed ground from lowlands to near timberline, and is found extensively in the western half of the United States to New England and northward throughout Canada.

Botanical Description

Hookedspur violet is a common native perennial forb of the violet family (Violaceae) featuring short to elongate, slender rhizomes and growing to approximately 10.2 cm in height. Perennial herb arising from a rhizome, lacking stolons, with stems absent in spring but developing ascending, usually hairy aerial stems 2-10 cm tall as the season progresses. Basal leaves are egg-shaped to heart-shaped with blunt-toothed margins, blades 0.5-4 cm long that are smooth or hairy, and stalks 5-7 cm long. Plants typically produce round to oval 5.1 cm leaves from branched, hairy stems.

Erosion & Slope Control

Pruning & Management

Hookedspur violet prefers recently disturbed sites, and is easily outcompeted by taller or more vigorous plants. Management to control competition and prevent encroachment of trees may be necessary to ensure survival of this species on restoration sites.

Ethnobotany (Historical Records)

Food Uses

The leaves and flowers of all violets are edible, and can be eaten in salads, used as potherbs, or brewed as tea.

Edible Parts & Flavour

Flowers; Edible flowers.

Medicinal (historical, not medical advice)

Hookedspur violet was used in traditional medicine by the Blackfoot, Nuxalk (Bella Coola) and Tolowa tribes. Leaves and roots were used to treat stomach problems and asthma in children, and as a cataplasm to treat soreness and swelling. In addition, the Southern Dakelh (Carrier), and S'Klallam (Klallam) used the plant as an analgesic and gastrointestinal aid, and the Makah tribe used it as a gynecological aid.

Pets, Children & Livestock

Generally non-toxic; harvest away from roadsides and pesticides, and confirm species ID before consumption.

Warnings, Toxicity & Pests

This plant is covered with many types of insects, but no damage has been observed to established plants. Developing seed is highly predated by both birds and mice, and control measures are necessary to avoid loss of large amounts of seed in seed production fields.

Propagation & Seeds

Sowing Protocol

Propagation Methods

Seeds can be sown on restoration sites in the fall at a rate of 1.1 kg/ha, or 50 seeds per square foot. For best establishment of seed production fields, stratify seeds in a cooler, sow into plugs in a greenhouse in late winter, and transplant out in spring. Transplanting into fields covered in weed fabric containing holes on a 0.3 m by 0.3 m spacing is most efficient for weed control and ease of seed harvest. Established by seed or as plugs.
